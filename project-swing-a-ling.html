S Hotel · Montego Bay · Jamaica

Swing A Ling

An installation celebrating Charlie Ace and Swing A Ling, his mobile record shop, through a recreated vehicle and a portrait sculpture beside a sound system at S Hotel Montego Bay.

The project

Charlie Ace:
music on the move.

Charlie Ace was a Jamaican deejay and record seller who took music directly to the streets of Kingston. He converted a Morris 1000 van into Swing A Ling, a mobile shop carrying records to its customers.

Scheed Cole’s installation brings the vehicle and the man into three dimensions: hand-painted music signs and record imagery cover the recreated shop, while the portrait sculpture rests an arm on a sound system. Together, they recall the people, enterprise and everyday street life behind Jamaica’s music culture.

The story behind the work

Taking music
to the people.

Who was Charlie Ace?

Charlie Ace was a Jamaican deejay and recording artist whose career began in the late 1960s with producer Joe Gibbs. He later recorded with Lee “Scratch” Perry and other Jamaican producers. His work with Perry included Cow Thief Skank.

He also became known for selling records from a converted Morris 1000 van. Swing A Ling took new releases through Kingston, bringing the record shop directly to the street.

What was Swing A Ling?

Swing A Ling was a mobile record shop and recording operation. Its painted signs advertised records, tapes and a range of musical styles. It was both a place to buy music and a striking visual presence in the city.

The photographs made by Adrian Boot in 1973 document the vehicle, its displays and Charlie Ace. They preserve a view of Jamaican music culture beyond the stage: the shops, street sellers and sound equipment that connected recordings with listeners.

How does the installation relate?

At S Hotel Montego Bay, the recreated vehicle carries the name and visual language of Swing A Ling. The portrait sculpture presents Charlie Ace beside a speaker stack, recalling the relaxed leaning pose in the historical photograph.

The painted vehicle and sculptural portrait form one project by Scheed Cole. Together, they bring a scene from Jamaica’s musical past into a space visitors can encounter in person.

From photograph to installation

Adrian Boot photographed Charlie Ace and his mobile record shop in Kingston in 1973. The leaning pose, speaker stack and painted vehicle seen in this image are echoed in the installation at S Hotel.

The photograph connects the sculptural portrait to the original street setting, showing how the figure, shop and sound system belonged together.
